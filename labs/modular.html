The modular surface: j(τ) and CM points

The upper half-plane, tiled by copies of one triangle, coloured by the j-function. Then the special points where j is an algebraic integer, and Duke's theorem that they spread out evenly.

modular formsquadratic formsinteractive

The group $\mathrm{SL}_2(\mathbb{Z})$ acts on the upper half-plane $\mathbb{H} = \{\tau : \mathrm{Im}\,\tau > 0\}$ by $\tau \mapsto \frac{a\tau + b}{c\tau + d}$. Every orbit meets the fundamental domain $\mathcal{F} = \{|\mathrm{Re}\,\tau| \le \tfrac12,\ |\tau| \ge 1\}$, and the copies $\gamma\mathcal{F}$ tile $\mathbb{H}$. The applet draws $\mathbb{H}$ as the Poincaré disc via $w = \frac{\tau - i}{\tau + i}$, so the tiles become curvilinear triangles that shrink towards the boundary circle.

A function on $\mathbb{H}$ that is invariant under the whole group is a function on the tiling. The most important one is Klein's $j$-function, $$j(\tau) = \frac{E_4(\tau)^3}{\Delta(\tau)} = \frac1q + 744 + 196884\,q + 21493760\,q^2 + \cdots, \qquad q = e^{2\pi i \tau},$$ with $E_4 = 1 + 240\sum_{n} \sigma_3(n) q^n$ and $\Delta = q\prod_{n \ge 1}(1 - q^n)^{24}$. The colour is the phase of $j(\tau)$ and the shading follows $\log|j(\tau)|$. Because $j$ is invariant, the same pattern repeats in every tile. $\Delta$ is not invariant, it is a cusp form of weight $12$, $\Delta(\gamma\tau) = (c\tau + d)^{12}\Delta(\tau)$, and switching to it shows the phase twisting from tile to tile.

Full proof: every point can be moved into the fundamental domain

Proposition. For every $\tau \in \mathbb{H}$ there is $\gamma \in \mathrm{SL}_2(\mathbb{Z})$ with $\gamma\tau \in \mathcal{F}$.

Proof. Let $T\tau = \tau + 1$ and $S\tau = -1/\tau$. For $\gamma = \begin{pmatrix} a & b \\ c & d\end{pmatrix}$ we have $\mathrm{Im}(\gamma\tau) = \mathrm{Im}\,\tau / |c\tau + d|^2$. Since $c\tau + d$ runs over a lattice as $(c, d)$ ranges over $\mathbb{Z}^2$, only finitely many pairs have $|c\tau + d| \le 1$, so $\mathrm{Im}(\gamma\tau)$ attains a maximum over the orbit, say at $\tau' = \gamma\tau$. Apply a power of $T$ so that $|\mathrm{Re}\,\tau'| \le \tfrac12$; this does not change the imaginary part. If $|\tau'| < 1$, then $\mathrm{Im}(S\tau') = \mathrm{Im}\,\tau' / |\tau'|^2 > \mathrm{Im}\,\tau'$, contradicting maximality. So $|\tau'| \ge 1$ and $\tau' \in \mathcal{F}$. $\square$

The algorithm in the applet does exactly this: translate into the strip, invert if inside the unit circle, repeat. Each inversion strictly increases the imaginary part, and the argument above shows it stops.

CM points and Duke's theorem

A primitive positive definite binary quadratic form $ax^2 + bxy + cy^2$ of discriminant $b^2 - 4ac = -D$ has a root $\tau = \frac{-b + i\sqrt D}{2a}$ in $\mathbb{H}$, and equivalent forms give $\mathrm{SL}_2(\mathbb{Z})$-equivalent roots. Gauss's reduced forms, those with $|b| \le a \le c$, are exactly the ones whose root lies in $\mathcal{F}$, so the points in the "CM points" view are the elements of the class group, and their number is the class number $h(-D)$.

These points are special: the elliptic curve $\mathbb{C}/(\mathbb{Z} + \tau\mathbb{Z})$ has complex multiplication, and $j(\tau)$ is an algebraic integer of degree $h(-D)$. When $h = 1$ it is an ordinary integer, which is why $e^{\pi\sqrt{163}}$ is so close to one; see the post 163 and the class number one problem.

As $D$ grows the points multiply (roughly like $\sqrt D$) and fill $\mathcal{F}$ more and more evenly, with respect to the hyperbolic area $\frac{3}{\pi}\frac{dx\,dy}{y^2}$, not the flat one. That is Duke's theorem (1988). The readout tests one consequence: the share of points with $\mathrm{Im}\,\tau > 1.5$ should approach $\frac{3}{\pi}\int_{1.5}^{\infty} \frac{dy}{y^2} = \frac{2}{\pi} \approx 63.7\%$. Duke's proof needed a nontrivial bound for Fourier coefficients of modular forms of half-integral weight, due to Iwaniec, which is why the theorem waited until the late 1980s.

A regular pentagon from a strip of paper

November 2026origamigeometry≈ 6 min read

Cut a long strip of paper of constant width, say $2$ cm wide and $30$ cm long. Tie a simple overhand knot in it, the same knot you would tie in a piece of string, and then pull gently and press it flat so that no paper bulges. What you are holding is a regular pentagon, with the two loose ends of the strip sticking out. Try it before reading on: it takes a minute, and the result looks far too neat to be an accident.

What the flattened knot looks like

Look closely at the flat knot. The strip runs straight between folds, and each straight run covers a band of width $w$, the width of the strip, whose two edges lie along lines through vertices of the pentagon. Following the strip around, you find five such bands, one for each side: the band for side $CD$ is bounded by $CD$ itself and by the diagonal $BE$, and similarly for the other sides. So the knot gives us a convex pentagon with two properties:

  1. every side is parallel to the diagonal "across" from it: $AB \parallel EC$, $BC \parallel AD$, $CD \parallel BE$, $DE \parallel CA$, $EA \parallel DB$;
  2. the distance between each side and its parallel diagonal is the same number $w$.

These two facts alone force the pentagon to be regular. The pleasant surprise is that the proof uses nothing but areas of triangles.

Full proof: equal bands force a regular pentagon

Proposition. Let $ABCDE$ be a convex pentagon in which each side is parallel to the diagonal across from it, at the same distance $w$ for all five sides. Then $ABCDE$ is regular.

Step 1: the five "ear" triangles have equal area. Write $T_1 = [ABC]$, $T_2 = [BCD]$, $T_3 = [CDE]$, $T_4 = [DEA]$, $T_5 = [EAB]$ for the areas. Since $C$ and $E$ lie on a line parallel to $AB$, triangles $ABC$ and $ABE$ have the same base and height, so $T_1 = T_5$. In the same way $BC \parallel AD$ gives $[BCD] = [BCA]$, so $T_2 = T_1$; $CD \parallel BE$ gives $T_3 = T_2$; and $DE \parallel CA$ gives $T_4 = T_3$.

Step 2: the sides are equal. The vertex $C$ lies on the diagonal $EC$, at distance $w$ from line $AB$, so $T_1 = \tfrac12|AB|\,w$. Likewise $T_2 = \tfrac12|BC|\,w$, because $D$ lies on $AD$, at distance $w$ from $BC$; and so on around. Equal areas give $|AB| = |BC| = |CD| = |DE| = |EA| = s$.

Step 3: the angles are equal. Now $T_1 = \tfrac12 s^2 \sin B$, $T_2 = \tfrac12 s^2 \sin C$, and so on, so all five interior angles have the same sine: each is $\alpha$ or $180^\circ - \alpha$. If $k$ of them equal $\alpha$, the angle sum $540^\circ$ gives $k\alpha + (5-k)(180^\circ - \alpha) = 540^\circ$. Running through $k = 0, \dots, 5$, the only possibilities are all five angles equal to $108^\circ$, or four angles of $120^\circ$ and one of $60^\circ$. In the second case the exterior angles are $60^\circ, 60^\circ, 60^\circ, 60^\circ, 120^\circ$, so the five unit side vectors point in directions $0^\circ, 60^\circ, 120^\circ, 180^\circ, 240^\circ$ (up to rotation), and their sum is $e^{2\pi i \cdot 2/6} \ne 0$: the polygon would not close. So every angle is $108^\circ$, and the equilateral, equiangular pentagon is regular. $\square$

Variations

Tying more elaborate knots in a strip gives other flat polygons, and working out which ones are regular is a good exercise in the same area argument; the overhand knot is the simplest case. The strip width fixes the size: for the pentagon, the side length is $s = w / \sin 72^\circ \approx 1.05\,w$.

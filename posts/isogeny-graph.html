Supersingular isogeny graphs

November 2026elliptic curvesexpander graphs≈ 8 min read

Here is a graph whose vertices are elliptic curves. Fix a prime $p$ and look at the supersingular elliptic curves in characteristic $p$, up to isomorphism; each is determined by its $j$-invariant, which always lies in $\mathbb{F}_{p^2}$. Join two of them by an edge when there is an isogeny of degree $2$ between them, a map of curves that is also a group homomorphism with kernel of order $2$. Every curve has exactly three subgroups of order $2$, so every vertex has degree three.

How the picture was made

Two $j$-invariants $j_1, j_2$ are $2$-isogenous exactly when $\Phi_2(j_1, j_2) = 0$, where $\Phi_2$ is the classical modular polynomial $$\Phi_2(X, Y) = X^3 + Y^3 - X^2Y^2 + 1488(X^2Y + XY^2) - 162000(X^2 + Y^2) + 40773375\,XY + 8748000000(X + Y) - 157464000000000.$$ For $p \equiv 3 \pmod 4$ the curve $y^2 = x^3 + x$, with $j = 1728$, is supersingular. Starting there and repeatedly solving the cubic $\Phi_2(j, Y) = 0$ over $\mathbb{F}_{p^2}$ reaches every supersingular $j$-invariant, because the graph is connected. There are $\lfloor p/12 \rfloor + \varepsilon_p$ of them, with $\varepsilon_p \in \{0, 1, 2\}$ depending on $p \bmod 12$; for $p = 431$ that is $35 + 2 = 37$.

Why number theorists and cryptographers care

They are Ramanujan graphs. The adjacency matrix of a $(\ell + 1)$-regular graph has the trivial eigenvalue $\ell + 1$; a graph is Ramanujan if every other eigenvalue has absolute value at most $2\sqrt{\ell}$, the best possible spectral gap for a large regular graph. Pizer showed (1990) that supersingular $\ell$-isogeny graphs are Ramanujan, because their adjacency matrices are Hecke operators acting on modular forms of weight $2$ for $\Gamma_0(p)$, and Deligne's proof of the Ramanujan–Petersson conjecture bounds the eigenvalues. Random walks on such graphs mix as fast as theoretically possible, in about $\log p$ steps.

Paths are hard to find. Given two supersingular curves, finding an isogeny path between them seems to be hard even for quantum computers. Charles, Goren and Lauter (2009) built a hash function from walks on the graph, and Jao and De Feo (2011) a key exchange (SIDH, later SIKE). In 2022 Castryck and Decru broke SIDH in about an hour on a laptop, using a theorem of Kani on abelian surfaces; the attack exploits extra information that SIDH publishes, so the underlying path-finding problem is still believed hard, and schemes such as CSIDH and the signature scheme SQIsign survive.

They are quaternion algebras in disguise. Deuring showed that supersingular curves correspond to maximal orders in the quaternion algebra over $\mathbb{Q}$ ramified at $p$ and $\infty$, and the isogeny graph becomes a graph of ideal classes. This is one of the cleanest windows onto the Jacquet–Langlands correspondence.

Full proof: the curve y² = x³ + x is supersingular when p ≡ 3 mod 4

For a prime $p > 3$, an elliptic curve over $\mathbb{F}_p$ is supersingular exactly when its trace of Frobenius $a_p = p + 1 - \#E(\mathbb{F}_p)$ is divisible by $p$; by Hasse's bound $|a_p| \le 2\sqrt p < p$ this means $a_p = 0$.

Proposition. If $p \equiv 3 \pmod 4$, then $E : y^2 = x^3 + x$ has exactly $p + 1$ points over $\mathbb{F}_p$, so it is supersingular.

Proof. Counting points with the quadratic character $\chi$ (one point at infinity), $\#E(\mathbb{F}_p) = p + 1 + \sum_{x} \chi(x^3 + x)$. The polynomial $f(x) = x^3 + x$ is odd, so $\chi(f(-x)) = \chi(-1)\chi(f(x)) = -\chi(f(x))$, because $-1$ is a non-residue when $p \equiv 3 \pmod 4$. The terms for $x$ and $-x$ cancel in pairs, and the term $x = 0$ is $\chi(0) = 0$. So the sum vanishes and $\#E(\mathbb{F}_p) = p + 1$. $\square$

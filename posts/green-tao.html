Long progressions of primes

November 2026primesadditive combinatorics≈ 7 min read

The primes $3, 5, 7$ are equally spaced. So are $5, 11, 17, 23, 29$, and $7, 37, 67, 97, 127, 157$. With a little searching you find ten:

In 2004 Ben Green and Terence Tao proved that this never stops: the primes contain arithmetic progressions of every finite length. The theorem is one of the main reasons for Tao's Fields Medal in 2006. Yet it gives no practical way to find long examples. The longest progression of primes actually known has 27 terms; it was found in 2019 by a volunteer computing project (PrimeGrid), and the search for 28 is under way.

Where the proof comes from

In 1927 van der Waerden proved that if the integers are coloured with finitely many colours, one colour contains arbitrarily long progressions. In 1975 Szemerédi proved a far stronger "density" version: any set of integers with positive upper density contains arbitrarily long progressions. The primes have density zero, so Szemerédi's theorem does not apply to them directly.

Green and Tao's idea was a transference principle. They showed that Szemerédi's theorem holds not only inside the integers but inside any sufficiently "pseudorandom" set, and that the primes form a set of positive relative density inside such a pseudorandom set, built from the almost-primes using sieve weights of Goldston and Yıldırım. The proof combines ergodic theory (Furstenberg's proof of Szemerédi's theorem), Fourier analysis and sieve theory. Later, Green, Tao and Ziegler went further and counted the progressions: the number of $k$-term progressions of primes up to $N$ is asymptotic to an explicit constant times $N^2/\log^k N$.

What is still open

Erdős conjectured that any set $A$ of positive integers with $\sum_{a \in A} 1/a = \infty$ contains arbitrarily long progressions. The primes satisfy this (Euler), so Green–Tao is a special case, but the general statement is open even for progressions of length $4$. The case of length $3$ was proved by Bloom and Sisask in 2020.

Full proof: why the common difference must be a multiple of 210

Lemma. Let $a, a + d, \dots, a + (k-1)d$ be primes, all larger than $k$. Then every prime $q \le k$ divides $d$.

Proof. Suppose a prime $q \le k$ does not divide $d$. Then $d$ is invertible modulo $q$, so the $q$ numbers $a, a + d, \dots, a + (q-1)d$ are pairwise incongruent modulo $q$ and run through all residues. One of them is divisible by $q$. It is a prime larger than $k \ge q$, so it cannot be divisible by $q$. Contradiction. $\square$

For ten primes starting above $10$, the difference is therefore a multiple of $2 \cdot 3 \cdot 5 \cdot 7 = 210$, which is why $199 + 210k$ is the natural place to look. For $27$ terms, the difference must be divisible by every prime up to $23$, a number larger than $2 \times 10^8$, so the terms are enormous.

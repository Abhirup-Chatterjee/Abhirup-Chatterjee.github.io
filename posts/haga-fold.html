Folding thirds: Haga's theorem is a Möbius transformation

October 2026origamigeometry≈ 5 min read

Dividing a square sheet of paper in half is easy: fold one edge onto the other. Dividing it into exact thirds, with no ruler and no guessing, is a nicer puzzle. The Japanese biologist and origami researcher Kazuo Haga found a one-fold answer.

Fold the bottom-left corner of a square up to the midpoint of the top edge. The bottom edge of the folded flap now crosses the right edge exactly one third of the way up.

Below, the corner can go anywhere along the top edge. Drag the slider and watch where the flap meets the right side.

Dashed: the crease. Shaded: the folded flap. Blue: where the flap edge meets the right side, at height h.

t = 0.500 h = 0.333

The computation

Put the square at $[0,1]^2$ and send the corner $(0,0)$ to $(t,1)$. The crease is the perpendicular bisector of the segment joining these points: $$t x + y = \frac{1 + t^2}{2}.$$ Reflecting across it sends the bottom edge, which leaves $(0,0)$ in direction $(1,0)$, to a line that leaves $(t,1)$ in direction $$\frac{1}{1+t^2}\big(1 - t^2,\; -2t\big).$$ This line reaches the right edge $x = 1$ after travelling a distance $\frac{1+t^2}{1+t}$, at height $$h(t) = 1 - \frac{2t}{1+t} = \frac{1-t}{1+t}.$$ At $t = \tfrac12$ this gives $h = \tfrac13$, which is Haga's theorem. The triangle cut off in the top-right corner has legs $\tfrac12$ and $\tfrac23$ and hypotenuse $\tfrac56$: a $3$–$4$–$5$ triangle hiding in a single fold.

The pun is real

The map $h(t) = \frac{1-t}{1+t}$ is a Möbius transformation, and it is an involution: $$h(h(t)) = \frac{1 - \frac{1-t}{1+t}}{1 + \frac{1-t}{1+t}} = \frac{2t}{2} = t.$$ So the fold runs both ways. Sending the corner to $t = \tfrac13$ marks the right edge at $\tfrac12$, and in general $t = \tfrac1n$ marks $h = \frac{n-1}{n+1}$:

corner at $t$$1/2$$1/3$$1/4$$1/5$$1/n$
mark at $h$$1/3$$1/2$$3/5$$2/3$$\frac{n-1}{n+1}$

Since halving is free and $h$ sends rationals to rationals, repeated Haga folds and halvings produce many fractions from nothing but a square.

Something to try

Starting from the endpoints $0$ and $1$, which fractions can you reach using only the moves $x \mapsto \tfrac{x}{2}$, $x \mapsto \tfrac{x+1}{2}$ (fold to a known mark) and $x \mapsto \frac{1-x}{1+x}$? Can you reach $\tfrac17$?

Answer: yes, and in fact every fraction in [0, 1]

$\tfrac17 = h\big(\tfrac34\big)$, and $\tfrac34 = \tfrac{1/2 + 1}{2}$ with $\tfrac12 = \tfrac{0 + 1}{2}$, so three moves suffice. In general, run the moves backwards from a reduced fraction $a/b$. If $a$ and $b$ are both odd, then $a/b = h(x)$ with $x = \frac{(b-a)/2}{(b+a)/2}$, whose denominator $(b+a)/2$ is smaller than $b$. If $a$ is even, then $a/b$ is $\tfrac{x}{2}$ with $x = \tfrac{2a}{b}$ or $\tfrac{x+1}{2}$ with $x = \tfrac{2a - b}{b}$, whichever lies in $[0, 1]$; repeating this at most $\log_2 b$ times produces an odd numerator. If $b$ is even, a halving move undone gives denominator $b/2$. Each round strictly lowers the denominator, so by induction every fraction in $[0, 1]$ is reachable from $0$ and $1$. $\square$

Markov numbers and the uniqueness conjecture

November 2026Diophantine equationsopen problems≈ 7 min read

The equation $$x^2 + y^2 + z^2 = 3xyz$$ has the obvious solution $(1, 1, 1)$. Fix two of the variables, say $y$ and $z$; then it is a quadratic in $x$, and if $x$ is one root, the other is $x' = 3yz - x$, again a positive integer. This move, called Vieta jumping, turns one solution into three neighbours, and starting from $(1, 1, 1)$ it produces every solution, arranged in a tree.

The numbers that appear are the Markov numbers $1, 2, 5, 13, 29, 34, 89, 169, 194, 233, 433, 610, 985, \dots$ Down the right-hand edge of the tree, the triples $(1, 5, 13), (1, 13, 34), (1, 34, 89), \dots$ are made of odd-indexed Fibonacci numbers, and down the branch through $(2, 5, 29)$, the triples $(2, 29, 169), (2, 169, 985), \dots$ are made of odd-indexed Pell numbers.

Why Markov cared: badly approximable numbers

Hurwitz proved in 1891 that every irrational $\alpha$ has infinitely many fractions $p/q$ with $|\alpha - p/q| < \frac{1}{\sqrt5\,q^2}$, and that $\sqrt5$ is optimal because of the golden ratio. Remove the golden ratio and its equivalents, and the constant improves to $\sqrt8$, achieved by $\sqrt2$; remove those, and it improves to $\sqrt{221}/5$; and so on. In 1879–80 Andrey Markov found the whole sequence of these constants below $3$: $$\sqrt{9 - \frac{4}{m^2}}, \qquad m \text{ a Markov number},$$ each achieved by a quadratic irrational built from a Markov triple. So the Markov tree describes the worst-approximable numbers, which is why the golden ratio, at the top, is the hardest number of all to approximate.

The uniqueness conjecture

In 1913 Frobenius asked whether each Markov number is the largest element of exactly one Markov triple. If so, the Markov constants above would correspond to Markov numbers one-to-one. The conjecture has been checked for all Markov numbers up to enormous size and proved for prime powers (Baragar, Button, Schmutz and others), but the general case is still open after more than a century. Aigner's book, listed below, is devoted to it.

Full proof: every Markov triple comes from (1, 1, 1)

Proposition. Every solution of $x^2 + y^2 + z^2 = 3xyz$ in positive integers is reached from $(1, 1, 1)$ by Vieta jumps.

Proof. Take a solution $a \le b \le c$ other than $(1, 1, 1)$ and $(1, 1, 2)$. We show that jumping the largest entry, $c \mapsto c' = 3ab - c$, gives a positive solution with $c' < c$; repeating, the largest entry strictly decreases, so we must reach $(1, 1, 2)$ and then $(1, 1, 1)$.

The numbers $c$ and $c'$ are the roots of $f(t) = t^2 - 3ab\,t + a^2 + b^2$, so $cc' = a^2 + b^2 > 0$ and $c'$ is a positive integer. First, $b < c$: if $b = c$ the equation gives $a^2 = b^2(3a - 2)$, so $b \mid a$, hence $a = b = c$ and $3a^2 = 3a^3$, so $a = 1$, excluded. Now evaluate $f(b) = 2b^2 + a^2 - 3ab^2$. If $a \ge 2$, then $f(b) \le 3b^2 - 3ab^2 < 0$. If $a = 1$, then $f(b) = 1 - b^2 < 0$ because $b \ge 2$ (as $a = b = 1$ forces $c \in \{1, 2\}$). In either case $f(b) < 0$, so $b$ lies strictly between the two roots, and since $c > b$ is the larger root, $c' < b < c$. $\square$

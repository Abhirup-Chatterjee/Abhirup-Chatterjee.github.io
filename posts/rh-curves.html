Riemann hypothesis for curves, from the zeta function of 𝔽q[t] upward

November 2026function fieldszeta functions≈ 9 min read

Over the integers, the Riemann hypothesis is the most famous open problem in mathematics. Over $\mathbb{F}_q[t]$ and its finite extensions it is a theorem, proved by André Weil in 1948. This post climbs from the simplest case, where the zeta function has no zeros at all, to curves of genus $g$, and explains why the resulting bound is the engine behind most of function-field number theory, including the questions on the Möbius function over polynomials that I work on.

The zeta function of 𝔽q[t]

Replace positive integers by monic polynomials and the absolute value $n$ by $|f| = q^{\deg f}$. The zeta function is $$\zeta_A(s) = \sum_{f \text{ monic}} \frac{1}{|f|^s} = \sum_{n \ge 0} \frac{q^n}{q^{ns}} = \frac{1}{1 - q^{1-s}},$$ because there are exactly $q^n$ monic polynomials of degree $n$. In the variable $u = q^{-s}$ it is the rational function $Z(u) = \frac{1}{1 - qu}$. Unique factorisation gives an Euler product over monic irreducibles $P$, $Z(u) = \prod_P (1 - u^{\deg P})^{-1}$, and comparing the two expressions gives the prime polynomial theorem: the number $\pi_q(n)$ of monic irreducibles of degree $n$ satisfies $$\pi_q(n) = \frac1n\sum_{d \mid n}\mu(d)\, q^{n/d} = \frac{q^n}{n} + O\!\left(\frac{q^{n/2}}{n}\right).$$ The error term has size $\sqrt{q^n}$, exactly what the Riemann hypothesis predicts for the integers, and here it comes for free because $Z(u)$ has no zeros. This $\zeta_A$ is really the zeta function of the projective line over $\mathbb{F}_q$, with the point at infinity removed; adding it back multiplies by $\frac{1}{1-u}$.

Full proof: the prime polynomial theorem

Proof. Take logarithmic derivatives of $\frac{1}{1 - qu} = \prod_P (1 - u^{\deg P})^{-1}$ and multiply by $u$: $$\frac{qu}{1 - qu} = \sum_P \frac{\deg P\, u^{\deg P}}{1 - u^{\deg P}} = \sum_P \sum_{k \ge 1} \deg P\, u^{k\deg P}.$$ Comparing coefficients of $u^n$ gives $q^n = \sum_{d \mid n} d\,\pi_q(d)$. Möbius inversion yields $n\pi_q(n) = \sum_{d \mid n}\mu(d)q^{n/d}$. The term $d = 1$ is $q^n$. The other terms are $\pm q^{k}$ for distinct exponents $k = n/d \le n/2$, so their total size is at most $\sum_{k \le n/2} q^k \le \frac{q}{q-1}\,q^{n/2} \le 2q^{n/2}$. Hence $|n\pi_q(n) - q^n| \le 2q^{n/2}$. $\square$

Curves of genus g

Now let $C$ be a smooth projective curve over $\mathbb{F}_q$ of genus $g$, for example the hyperelliptic curve $y^2 = f(x)$ with $f$ squarefree of degree $2g + 1$. Counting its points over each extension $\mathbb{F}_{q^r}$ defines $$Z_C(u) = \exp\!\left(\sum_{r \ge 1} \frac{\#C(\mathbb{F}_{q^r})}{r}\,u^r\right).$$ Artin conjectured in his 1924 thesis (for hyperelliptic curves) and F. K. Schmidt proved in general that $$Z_C(u) = \frac{L_C(u)}{(1 - u)(1 - qu)}, \qquad L_C(u) = \prod_{j=1}^{2g}(1 - \alpha_j u),$$ with $L_C$ a polynomial of degree $2g$ with integer coefficients, and that $Z_C$ satisfies a functional equation under $u \mapsto 1/qu$. Taking logarithms, the point counts are $$\#C(\mathbb{F}_{q^r}) = q^r + 1 - \sum_{j=1}^{2g}\alpha_j^{\,r}.$$ The Riemann hypothesis for $C$ is the statement $|\alpha_j| = \sqrt q$ for every $j$, equivalently that the zeros of $Z_C(q^{-s})$ lie on $\mathrm{Re}\,s = \tfrac12$. Hasse proved it for elliptic curves in 1933, Weil for all curves in 1948, using the geometry of the Jacobian and intersection theory on $C \times C$. Stepanov (1969) and Bombieri (1973) later found an elementary proof using auxiliary polynomials, and Deligne (1974) proved the analogue for varieties of every dimension.

The consequence everybody uses is the Hasse–Weil bound $$\big|\#C(\mathbb{F}_{q^r}) - q^r - 1\big| \le 2g\,q^{r/2}.$$ You can watch the zeros $\alpha_j/\sqrt q$ sit on the unit circle, and their statistics, in the zeros on the unit circle lab.

Why it is the engine

Most counting problems over $\mathbb{F}_q[t]$ reduce to bounding a character sum, and a character sum is the error term in a point count on some curve. Three examples:

  • Primes in progressions. For a modulus $m \in \mathbb{F}_q[t]$ and a nontrivial Dirichlet character $\chi$, the $L$-function $L(u, \chi)$ is a polynomial of degree less than $\deg m$, and its zeros satisfy the Riemann hypothesis because it divides the zeta function of a curve (a cyclic cover of the line). So primes are equidistributed in residue classes with square-root error: the full generalised Riemann hypothesis, proved.
  • Character sums. Weil's bound $\big|\sum_{x \in \mathbb{F}_q}\chi(f(x))\big| \le (\deg f - 1)\sqrt q$ for a multiplicative character of order $d$ and $f$ not a $d$-th power is the hyperelliptic Riemann hypothesis in disguise; the Kloosterman paths lab shows the additive version.
  • Large-$q$ limits. As $q \to \infty$ with the degree fixed, Deligne's equidistribution theorem turns the Riemann hypothesis into statements about random matrices. This is how Carmon and Rudnick proved the function-field Chowla conjecture in that limit.

What the Riemann hypothesis does not give is uniformity when $q$ is fixed and the degree grows, because the number of zeros grows with the genus. That regime, fixed $q$ and large degree, is where the hard open problems live, and where the analogy with the integers is closest.

A first look at multiple zeta values

November 2026zeta valuesmodular forms≈ 8 min read

In December 2025 IMSc hosted an IWM Research in Groups week on classical and finite multiple zeta values. These notes are my attempt at the first lecture I would have liked to hear: what multiple zeta values are, the surprising identities between them, and the unexpected way modular forms count those identities.

Definitions and Euler's identity

For integers $s_1 \ge 2$ and $s_2, \dots, s_k \ge 1$, the multiple zeta value is $$\zeta(s_1, \dots, s_k) = \sum_{n_1 > n_2 > \cdots > n_k \ge 1} \frac{1}{n_1^{s_1} n_2^{s_2} \cdots n_k^{s_k}}.$$ The weight is $s_1 + \dots + s_k$ and the depth is $k$. Depth one gives the Riemann zeta values. Euler already knew that the double sum $\zeta(2, 1) = \sum_{n > m \ge 1} \frac{1}{n^2 m}$ equals $\zeta(3)$, an identity between objects of different depths and the same weight.

Full proof: ζ(2, 1) = ζ(3)

Proof. Evaluate $S = \sum_{n, m \ge 1} \frac{1}{nm(n+m)}$ (a convergent sum of positive terms) in two ways.

First, $\frac{1}{nm} = \frac{1}{n+m}\left(\frac1n + \frac1m\right)$, so by symmetry $S = 2\sum_{n, m} \frac{1}{n(n+m)^2}$. Setting $k = n + m$, this is $2\sum_{k \ge 2}\frac{1}{k^2}\sum_{n=1}^{k-1}\frac1n = 2\zeta(2, 1)$.

Second, $\frac{1}{m(n+m)} = \frac1n\left(\frac1m - \frac{1}{n+m}\right)$, and $\sum_{m \ge 1}\left(\frac1m - \frac{1}{n+m}\right) = H_n = 1 + \frac12 + \dots + \frac1n$ (telescoping). So $S = \sum_{n}\frac{H_n}{n^2} = \sum_n \frac{H_{n-1}}{n^2} + \sum_n \frac{1}{n^3} = \zeta(2, 1) + \zeta(3)$.

Comparing, $2\zeta(2,1) = \zeta(2,1) + \zeta(3)$, so $\zeta(2, 1) = \zeta(3)$. $\square$

Two ways to multiply

Products of multiple zeta values are again combinations of multiple zeta values, in two different ways. Multiplying the defining series and splitting according to whether the indices are equal gives the stuffle (or harmonic) product, for example $$\zeta(a)\,\zeta(b) = \zeta(a, b) + \zeta(b, a) + \zeta(a + b).$$ Writing each value as an iterated integral on $[0, 1]$ (Kontsevich), products of integrals over simplices give the shuffle product, for example $\zeta(2)\zeta(2) = 2\zeta(2,2) + 4\zeta(3,1)$. Comparing the two expansions of the same product yields linear relations, the double shuffle relations; here $\zeta(2)^2 = 2\zeta(2,2) + \zeta(4)$ from the stuffle side, and with $\zeta(2) = \pi^2/6$, $\zeta(4) = \pi^4/90$ one gets $\zeta(2,2) = \pi^4/120$ and $\zeta(3,1) = \pi^4/360$.

How many are there?

Let $d_k$ be the dimension of the $\mathbb{Q}$-span of the multiple zeta values of weight $k$ (with $d_0 = 1$, $d_1 = 0$, $d_2 = 1$). Zagier conjectured that $$d_k = d_{k-2} + d_{k-3}, \qquad \text{so } d_k = 1, 0, 1, 1, 1, 2, 2, 3, 4, 5, 7, 9, 12, \dots,$$ far fewer than the $2^{k-2}$ values of weight $k$. Terasoma and Deligne–Goncharov proved that $d_k$ is at most this number, using mixed Tate motives, and Brown (2012) showed that the values $\zeta(s_1, \dots, s_k)$ with every $s_i \in \{2, 3\}$ span everything (Hoffman's conjecture). The lower bound is out of reach: we cannot even prove that $\zeta(5)/\pi^5$ is irrational.

Double zeta values and modular forms

In depth two and even weight $k$, consider the double zeta values with both arguments odd. Gangl, Kaneko and Zagier (2006) found that the linear relations among them, beyond the obvious ones, are counted by cusp forms: their number is the dimension of the space $S_k(\mathrm{SL}_2(\mathbb{Z}))$, through the period polynomials of Eichler, Shimura and Manin. The first cusp form is $\Delta$, in weight $12$, and correspondingly weight $12$ has the first exotic relation, $$28\,\zeta(9, 3) + 150\,\zeta(7, 5) + 168\,\zeta(5, 7) = \frac{5197}{691}\,\zeta(12).$$ The $691$ is the numerator of the Bernoulli number $B_{12}$, the same prime that appears in Ramanujan's congruence $\tau(n) \equiv \sigma_{11}(n) \pmod{691}$. This link between modular forms and multiple zeta values, explained conceptually by Brown and others through the motivic Lie algebra, was one of the main themes of the week.

Banach–Mazur games, or how to win at topology

November 2026gamestopology≈ 7 min read

This is a friendly version of a talk I gave at IMSc in January 2022. The idea is to turn a topological property of a set into a game between two players, and then to read the property off from who has a winning strategy.

The game

Fix a set $A \subseteq \mathbb{R}$, the target. Two players, Alice and Bob, take turns choosing open intervals, each inside the previous one: $$I_0 \supseteq J_0 \supseteq I_1 \supseteq J_1 \supseteq I_2 \supseteq \cdots$$ Alice chooses the $I_n$ and Bob the $J_n$. Bob wins if the intersection of all the intervals is contained in $A$; Alice wins otherwise. (The intersection may be a single point or even empty; an empty intersection counts as a win for Bob.)

Stanisław Mazur invented the game and asked, in problem 43 of the Scottish Book (1935), for which sets $A$ Bob has a winning strategy. Banach answered the question, though his proof was never published; John Oxtoby gave the first published proof in 1957.

Theorem (Banach–Mazur–Oxtoby). Bob has a winning strategy if and only if $A$ is comeager, that is, $A$ contains a countable intersection of dense open sets.

So "Bob can force the outcome into $A$" is exactly "$A$ is large in the sense of Baire category". For instance, Bob can make sure the final point is irrational: the irrationals are the intersection of the dense open sets $\mathbb{R} \setminus \{r_n\}$, where $r_1, r_2, \dots$ lists the rationals. Concretely, at move $n$ Bob shrinks Alice's interval to avoid $r_n$. He can also force the final point to be a Liouville number, because the Liouville numbers form a comeager set, even though they have measure zero. Category and measure disagree about which sets are large, and the game sees category.

Full proof: if A is comeager, Bob wins

Proof. Suppose $A \supseteq \bigcap_{n \ge 0} D_n$ with each $D_n$ open and dense. Bob's strategy: when Alice plays $I_n$, the set $I_n \cap D_n$ is nonempty (because $D_n$ is dense) and open, so it contains an open interval; Bob plays such an interval $J_n \subseteq I_n \cap D_n$. Then every point of $\bigcap_n J_n$ lies in every $D_n$, hence in $A$. $\square$

The converse, that a winning strategy for Bob forces $A$ to be comeager, is the substantial half. The idea is to unfold Bob's strategy into a tree: at stage $n$, choose a maximal family of disjoint positions reachable after $n$ rounds whose union is dense, which is possible because there are only countably many rational intervals; the union $U_n$ of Bob's answers is open and dense, and $\bigcap_n U_n \subseteq A$ because every point of it is the outcome of a play following the strategy. The details are in Oxtoby's book and in Kechris, Theorem 8.33.

A bonus: the same strategy proves the Baire category theorem for $\mathbb{R}$. Suppose $\mathbb{R} = \bigcup_n F_n$ with each $F_n$ closed and nowhere dense. Then the sets $D_n = \mathbb{R}\setminus F_n$ are open and dense, and Bob plays as above, also making the closure of $J_n$ lie inside $I_n \cap D_n$ and its length at most $1/n$. The intervals then shrink to a single point (completeness of $\mathbb{R}$), which lies in every $D_n$ and hence in no $F_n$: a contradiction. So $\mathbb{R}$ is not a countable union of nowhere dense sets.

Choquet's games and Polish spaces

The same idea works in any topological space $X$, with nonempty open sets in place of intervals. In the Choquet game Bob simply wants the intersection to be nonempty. In the strong Choquet game, Alice must also name a point $x_n$ inside each of her open sets, and Bob's next set must contain $x_n$. Choquet proved a remarkable characterisation: a nonempty, second countable, metrizable space is Polish, meaning it admits a complete metric, if and only if Bob has a winning strategy in the strong Choquet game. Completeness of a metric, a metric property, becomes a property of a game that mentions only open sets.

Games of this kind run through descriptive set theory: determinacy of infinite games (Gale and Stewart, Martin's Borel determinacy theorem) explains why "nice" sets of reals have the regularity properties one expects, and the puzzle shelf has a finite cousin, the strategy-stealing argument for Chomp.
